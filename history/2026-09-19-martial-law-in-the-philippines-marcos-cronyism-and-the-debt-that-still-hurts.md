On September 21, nineteenth seventy two, President Ferdinand Marcos issued Proclamation Number Ten Eighty One , placing the Philippines under martial law. Marcos asserted that the nation faced mounting threats from widespread civil unrest and a growing communist insurgency, and he presented martial law as a measure necessary to restore order and protect national security. The proclamation granted the executive sweeping powers: the suspension of the writ of habeas corpus, censorship of the press, control over assemblies, and broad authority to detain persons without charge. These measures were justified by the administration as temporary steps to reestablish stability, but in practice they produced a prolonged period of authoritarian rule.

One of the most prominent victims of the new regime’s crackdown was Senator Benigno Ninoy Aquino Junior a vocal critic and leading opposition figure. Aquino had long challenged Marcos’s policies and growing consolidation of power; after martial law was declared he was arrested and imprisoned for years on charges related to subversion. His detention became a focal point for domestic and international concern about human rights abuses in the Philippines, symbolizing the broader silencing of dissent under Marcos.

Under the cover of martial law, the Marcos administration radically reshaped political life. Opposition parties were dismantled or co-opted, activists and journalists faced intimidation, and key institutions such as the judiciary and the legislature, and the media—were brought under tight state control. The climate of fear and heavy-handed policing curtailed civic freedoms and left ordinary citizens with fewer avenues to contest government action.

Simultaneously, martial law opened opportunities for large-scale corruption and economic mismanagement. Close associates and family members of Marcos consolidated control over lucrative sectors of the economy in banking and real estate and natural resources, and strategic corporations—often through favorable contracts, crony appointments, and opaque privatizations. State resources and government contracts were redirected to benefit political allies. This concentration of economic power facilitated what many historians and economists describe as systematic plunder, as wealth was extracted from public coffers and national assets transferred into private hands associated with the regime.

The economic consequences were severe and lasting. While Marcos cited development and modernization as rationales for authoritarian measures, public debt rose sharply during his tenure, and foreign loans were frequently misallocated. Infrastructure projects and state-sponsored programs sometimes served as vehicles for kickbacks and inflated contracts rather than sustained public benefit. The result was a growing disparity between a privileged political-economic elite and the broader population, many of whom continued to suffer poverty and declining living standards.

The repression and the economic predations of the martial-law period prompted both domestic resistance and international criticism. Over the decade that followed, clandestine movements and the student groups, church leaders, and human rights activists kept pressure on the regime. The death of opposition figure Ninoy Aquino, combined with mounting evidence of corruption and abuses, helped to galvanize a broad-based movement that ultimately contributed to the democratic uprising in nineteen eighty six
Decades after the proclamation of martial law, the period remains a defining and contested chapter in Philippine history—remembered for its suspension of civil liberties, the suffering endured by countless victims, and the deep economic distortions created by cronyism and plunder. 
Many Filipinos ask why basic commodities are more expensive today than during the martial-law years. One important factor is the country’s long-term debt burden. During the Marcos administration, the government borrowed extensively, and those loans accumulated interest. Succeeding administrations inherited the responsibility of repaying these obligations.

In the 2025 national budget, approximately ₱848.03 billion, or 13.3% of total national government expenditures, was allocated for interest payments alone. When principal repayments are included, total debt-service expenses reached about ₱2.051 trillion.

The government raises revenue through various taxes, including the 12% Value Added Tax imposed on most taxable goods, services, and imports.
