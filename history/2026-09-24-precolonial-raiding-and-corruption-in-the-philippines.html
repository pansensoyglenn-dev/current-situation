From Precolonial Raiding to Modern Corruption in the Philippines

The history of the Philippines before Spanish colonization was not defined only by peaceful trade and village life. Many coastal and island communities also practiced raiding, warfare, tribute-taking, and the capture of people. Although modern corruption is not the same as precolonial raiding, both can be examined through a common question: How do powerful groups obtain wealth when institutions are weak and ordinary people have limited protection?

Raiding in Precolonial Society

Before the arrival of Spain, the Philippine archipelago was composed of many relatively autonomous communities often led by a datu. These communities traded with neighboring settlements and foreign merchants, but they also competed for land, labor, prestige, and access to valuable waterways.

Historical research indicates that raiding was part of the political and economic life of some Philippine chiefdoms. Raiding expeditions could obtain goods, captives, and slaves, while successful leaders gained prestige and strengthened their authority.

A chief’s power was connected to his ability to protect and support his followers. Trade, warfare, redistribution of wealth, and public feasting were among the ways leaders maintained loyalty. In this system, raiding was not necessarily viewed by participants in the same way that modern society views robbery; it could be associated with warfare, political rivalry, and the duties of a powerful leader.

However, this does not mean that precolonial communities were uniformly violent or primitive. Trade networks connected the islands to China, Southeast Asia, and other regions. Communities developed agriculture, boat-building, local laws, religious practices, and systems of social organization. Raiding existed alongside cooperation, commerce, diplomacy, and cultural exchange.

Captives, Debt, and Power

One important consequence of raiding was the capture of people. Some captives became dependents or slaves, while other forms of servitude resulted from debt. Historical descriptions indicate that social dependency could have different levels, ranging from household service to obligations connected with debt and labor.

This system gave powerful families access to labor and increased their wealth. It also created a deep inequality between those who controlled land, weapons, trade, and political authority and those who had fewer resources. In this sense, raiding was not simply an act of violence; it was also a mechanism for transferring wealth and human labor from weaker groups to stronger ones.

The Sulu region provides another example of the close relationship among trade, warfare, and maritime raiding. Raiding was part of the political and economic structure of the region, while later European powers increasingly labeled their enemies “pirates.” Colonial expansion also changed and intensified patterns of maritime raiding.

This historical complexity is important. The word piracy was sometimes used by colonial authorities to describe enemies, while similar forms of violence could be presented as legitimate warfare when practiced by states. Therefore, historians must consider who created the records and whose interests those records represented.

From Taking by Force to Taking Through Office

Modern corruption is different from precolonial raiding. A raider used weapons, ships, and physical force to take goods or captives. A corrupt official may misuse a government position, manipulate a contract, demand a bribe, or divert public money. One is an act of armed seizure; the other is an abuse of public authority.

Despite this difference, the two systems can be compared at the level of political behavior. Both involve powerful individuals or groups using authority, connections, or coercion to transfer resources away from the wider community. In precolonial raiding, wealth could be taken from another settlement. In modern corruption, public funds can be redirected from roads, schools, hospitals, irrigation systems, or flood-control projects.

The comparison should not be used to claim that Filipinos are naturally corrupt or that present-day corruption is an unchanged survival from the precolonial past. Such an argument would be historically weak and unfair. Corruption is shaped by institutions, political incentives, enforcement, economic inequality, and the behavior of individuals and organizations.

The Present Corruption Problem

Recent corruption indicators show that the problem remains serious. In the 2025 Corruption Perceptions Index, released in 2026, the Philippines received a score of 32 out of 100 and ranked 120th among 182 countries and territories. The score declined from 33 in the previous edition. [web:1]

The index reflects perceptions of public-sector corruption, including concerns related to bribery, misuse of public resources, red tape, and nepotistic appointments. Reports also connected the Philippines’ lower ranking with controversy surrounding alleged irregularities in flood-control projects. [web:1]

For ordinary Filipinos, corruption is not an abstract statistical issue. It can appear in practical and painful forms:

  • A farmer may wait for an irrigation project that is delayed, poorly built, or never completed.
  • A community may remain vulnerable to flooding because funds intended for protection were misused.
  • A student may study in an overcrowded classroom while public money is lost through inflated contracts.
  • A patient may struggle to receive proper health services because resources do not reach the intended facility.
  • A small business owner may face unnecessary demands for payments before receiving a permit or service.

In these situations, public money functions like the goods taken during an old raid: it is removed from the community, but the method is now hidden behind paperwork, contracts, political influence, or bureaucratic authority.

The Role of Patronage

One link between older political structures and modern corruption can be found in personal loyalty. In many precolonial communities, leaders depended on relatives, warriors, dependents, and followers. Political authority was often personal rather than exercised through a modern impersonal bureaucracy.

Modern Philippine politics still sometimes operates through patronage networks. A politician may distribute jobs, contracts, aid, or favors to supporters, while beneficiaries are expected to return political loyalty. This does not mean that patronage is identical to the datu system. The Philippines now has a constitution, laws, courts, elections, government agencies, and public auditing institutions. Nevertheless, when personal loyalty becomes more important than public rules, government resources can be treated as the property of a political group.

This is where corruption becomes especially damaging. It transforms public office into a private source of wealth and influence. Instead of serving as a trustee of the people, the official behaves like a ruler extracting resources from subjects.

A Historical Warning

The history of precolonial raiding offers a warning about what happens when power is not sufficiently restrained. A strong leader could gain wealth by organizing violence against weaker communities. In the modern state, the same basic imbalance can appear when officials control public resources while citizens lack information, access, or effective legal remedies.

The solution, however, is not to romanticize the past or to condemn traditional societies. Precolonial Filipinos developed systems of cooperation, trade, leadership, and mutual obligation, just as they experienced conflict and inequality. The useful lesson is that communities need institutions capable of limiting the abuse of power.

These institutions include:

  • Transparent public bidding and accessible government records.
  • Independent auditing and prosecution of corruption cases.
  • Protection for whistleblowers and investigative journalists.
  • Stronger citizen participation in local projects.
  • Clear monitoring of infrastructure and agricultural programs.
  • Public education that explains how taxes and government budgets are spent.
  • Consistent punishment regardless of political influence.

Conclusion

Precolonial raiding and present-day corruption belong to different historical worlds, but they can be connected through the problem of unequal power. Earlier raiders acquired wealth through force, captives, and tribute; modern corrupt officials may acquire it through public office, hidden deals, and the diversion of state resources.
