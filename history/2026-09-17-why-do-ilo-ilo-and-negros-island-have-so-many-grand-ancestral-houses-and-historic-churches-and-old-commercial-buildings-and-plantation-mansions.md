Ilo-Ilo and Negros are located in Western Visayas, but their historical development followed different yet connected paths.

Before the sugar boom, many communities in the region depended mainly on farming and fishing and local trade, and other forms of livelihood.

Meanwhile in the Caribbean, Santo - Domingo -now Haiti- was once a leading sugar producer, but a massive slave uprising destroyed many plantations and caused sugar output to collapse. By contrast, the sugar boom in the central Philippines grew from the opening of Ilo-Ilo to world trade. From the Eighteenth Fifthtease  British vice-consul in Ilo-Ilo Nicholas Loney promoted modern sugar-processing equipment and credit, and improved milling methods and encouraged Ilo-Ilo planters to relocate to Negros, where large tracts of inexpensive, fertile land were available. With Ilo-Ilo’s port serving as a gateway to international markets, planters could export sugar profitably, spurring rapid expansion of plantations and mills in Negros and  Ilo-Ilo from the late Eighteenth Fifties onward.

The opening of Western Visayas to global trade spurred demand for new infrastructure.Ilo-Ilo’s port required warehouses for imports and exports and offices for merchants and trading firms, transforming the city into a commercial gateway. Meanwhile,Negros became a leading sugar-producing region. The wealth generated by the sugar economy created powerful landowning elites who built grand mansions, funded churches, and renovated streets and public buildings in European architectural styles. These elites — the Principalia — held economic power as well as social and political influence, shaping Philippine society. Notably, many of the wealthy and influential families came from Ilo-Ilo and Negros rather than from Manila.

Their wealth and connections also influenced the culinary culture of the region. Through trade, 
Ilo-Ilo and Negros gained access to imported ingredients, Spanish cooking methods, and new dining traditions, which local cooks adapted to native products and tastes. Wealthy families served elaborate dishes during fiestas, weddings, religious celebrations, and other social gatherings, helping establish food as an expression of hospitality, status, and regional identity. Valenciana,influenced by Spanish paella, reflected Ilo-Ilo’s commercial links and the availability of imported ingredients, while lechon became a prominent centerpiece of wealthy celebrations and public feasts. In Negros, the adaptation of beef-based cooking and local souring ingredients produced dishes such as kansi,a soup combining the richness of bulalo with the distinctive sour flavor of batwan. In this way, the same trade networks and elite society that shaped the architecture and economy, and politics of Ilo-Ilo and Negros also helped create and popularize their distinctive culinary traditions.
