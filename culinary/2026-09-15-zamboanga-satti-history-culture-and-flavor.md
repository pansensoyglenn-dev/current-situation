You step into Zamboanga City and a few things strike at once: the barter markets that hum with bargaining voices, the neat rows of sardine cans stacked like small, metallic altars, the musical lilt of Chavacano — that Spanish-born Creole that makes Zamboanga the only Latin city in Asia. But for many who live here, the true introduction is through the nose: the smoky, spicy scent of satti rising from a roadside cart. It is a simple plate — skewered meat, rice, and a lacquer of red sauce — and yet it carries the map of centuries in its taste.
Satti did not appear from nowhere. It is the child of tides and tongues. Long before the modern nation-states, boats threaded the Sulu Sea and threaded Zamboanga’s shores: Tausug lakans and Sama-Bajau praus, Malay and Indonesian traders with woven sails, merchants from Gujarat and the Arabian Sea, all following wind and profit. They brought spices and stories, cooking methods and words. The same maritime highways that brought silk and porcelain also carried skewers and sauces, and so a version of satay found its way into local hearths.
In the kitchens of Tausug and Sama families, skewered meat was grilled over coals, turning slowly while women and men bunched coconut leaves and ground chilies on wooden boards. Malay techniques—marinades of turmeric and tamarind—mingled with Indian ways of spice-wearing meat, while Arabic traders introduced new pepper blends and dried limes. Over time, these influences braided together with Spanish-era ingredients and the region’s own flavors. Out of that weaving came satti: not the peanut-dressed satay of Java or the sweet soy-laden skewers of the archipelago, but a saltier, fierier child, lacquered in a red gravy that commands attention.
